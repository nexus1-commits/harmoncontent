Service Page or Blog Post? Where Should Your Customer’s Question Go?

By Harmon Content · Published 14 October 2026

A customer asks, “Do I need to move the furniture before you arrive?” You could turn that into a blog post. But if the answer is two useful sentences, the better home may be the service page they are already reading.

Put the facts someone needs to choose or book your service on the relevant service page. Write a separate article when the question needs its own explanation, comparison or checklist. Use both only when each page has a clear job.

This is a practical planning approach for small service businesses in Canada and the US, not a ranking formula.

The quick decision

  • Service page: “What does your business offer, and is it right for me?”
  • Short answer or FAQ on that page: “What specific detail do I need before contacting you?”
  • Blog post: “Help me understand my options or work through a longer question.”

Before creating anything, check whether an existing page already answers the question. Improving that answer may be more useful than publishing another version.

Keep essential buying details on the service page

A service page helps someone decide whether to enquire. It should not send them on a scavenger hunt for the terms of the service.

Keep these details close to the offer:

  • The work you do and who it suits.
  • What is included, excluded or quoted separately.
  • Your actual service area and any relevant limits.
  • How pricing or a quote works, using confirmed information.
  • What the customer needs to provide and what happens next.

If a condition could change someone’s decision to contact you, give it a clear home here. A short “Before your appointment” section can be better than a separate article that customers never find.

For businesses serving different markets, be specific. Label a published price as C$ or US$ where needed, and explain the area you actually serve. Do not imply cross-border availability simply because your website can be read in both countries.

Need help with the offer itself? Service-page copywriting focuses on making one important page clearer.

Give a longer question its own article

A blog post earns its place when it helps a reader do something beyond understanding your basic offer.

Useful topics might compare two approaches, walk through a preparation checklist, or explain the information needed to make a sensible decision. The article should still help someone who is not ready to buy from you today.

Use your business knowledge to add substance: the choices customers overlook, the questions worth asking and the situations where an option is unsuitable. Confirm those details before publishing. Do not stretch a short answer with a long introduction just to reach a target length.

Google’s people-first content guidance asks whether a page helps its intended audience and adds useful original value. It also says Google has no preferred word count. Length should follow the question, not a supposed SEO minimum.

Three questions that can belong in both places

These are fictional planning examples, not client results or claims about what every provider includes.

“What affects the price?”

On the service page: Explain how this business prepares a quote and which confirmed factors affect it. State any applicable minimums or exclusions rather than hiding them in an old post.

In an article: Help readers compare quotes. For a painting business, a useful guide could prompt them to check the rooms and surfaces covered, preparation, materials and cleanup. The business must verify which details apply to its own work.

The distinction: The page explains your offer. The article gives the customer a comparison method.

“What should I do before the appointment?”

On the service page: Keep mandatory preparation instructions easy to find. If the answer is short, stop there.

In an article: Create a checklist only if there is enough useful detail. A home-organizing business might explain how to choose a first area, describe the help wanted and identify items the customer does not want handled.

The distinction: Essential instructions stay beside the booking information; optional preparation gets room to breathe.

“Which service option should I choose?”

On the service page: Summarize the real options and their limits.

In an article: Explain the trade-offs with worked situations. A cleaning business could compare recurring visits with a one-time appointment, provided the descriptions match its actual scope. Do not invent a universal definition of “deep cleaning.”

The distinction: Readers get a quick comparison first, with a deeper explanation available when needed.

Connect the pages without repeating everything

Give the short answer on the service page, then link to the fuller guide where it genuinely helps. In the guide, link back to the relevant service with a natural next step. Avoid copying the whole article onto the service page.

Use link wording that names the destination, such as “compare our cleaning options,” rather than “click here.” Google’s link guidance recommends descriptive, contextual links and a link to each important page from somewhere else on the site.

The same principle applies to AI-search preparation: make the information useful and findable. Google says its usual SEO fundamentals apply to AI Overviews and AI Mode; no special AI file or schema is required. That guidance is specific to Google, and it does not guarantee indexing or inclusion.

Make a small question map

Choose three questions you hear in enquiries. For each, record:

  1. The customer’s decision: What are they trying to work out?
  2. The confirmed answer: What does your business actually do?
  3. The existing home: Which page should answer it now?
  4. The extra value: Would a separate guide add something useful?
  5. The next step: What should the reader be able to do afterward?

Use the question, not the customer’s identifying details. Start with the most important missing answer, not a whole new publishing calendar.

Check the result on a phone

Before publishing, make sure:

  • The title and first paragraph explain the page’s purpose.
  • Headings describe the sections and follow a sensible order.
  • Text, lists and buttons fit a narrow screen without sideways scrolling.
  • Link text makes sense, keyboard focus is visible, and the next step works.
  • Images have useful text alternatives when needed; important instructions are not image-only.
  • Service facts agree across the page, related articles and any structured data.

W3C’s writing guidance covers meaningful headings, links, image alternatives and clear instructions. These checks are a starting point, not a full accessibility audit.

Choose the smallest useful next step

If the answer belongs on a service page, improve that page. If it deserves a guide, give it a specific purpose and a relevant link. If the website already answers it well, there may be nothing new to publish.
