Before They Contact You: Make Your Enquiry Page Clearer

By Harmon Content · Published 28 October 2026

A visitor has read your service page and is ready to ask a question. Then the contact section says “Get started,” the form asks for a budget, and nobody explains what happens after sending it. The next writing task may be a few clearer sentences, rather than another blog post.

Explain what an enquiry starts, ask for the details you need for that first conversation, and describe the next step honestly. Make the wording agree with the service page and the form. A contact section can do this job without becoming a separate page.

This is a content review for small service businesses in Canada and the US. It offers practical editorial checks, not measured conversion improvements or a ranking formula.

Name the action the visitor is actually taking

First, decide what your form does. Does it send a question, request a quote, or complete a booking? Those are different actions.

Use a heading and button label that fit the real process. If someone still needs a conversation before an appointment is confirmed, “Book now” can set the wrong expectation. A label such as “Request a quote” is more specific when that is what the business actually provides.

Keep that promise consistent. The service page, the link leading to the form, the form heading and the confirmation should describe the same action. If the problem is finding the form in the first place, start with our internal link audit for small-business websites.

Answer three questions before the form

Give the visitor a short explanation of:

  1. Who should use it: The service or type of question this route is for.
  2. What to include: The basic details that help you understand the request.
  3. What happens next: How the business handles an enquiry and what remains unconfirmed.

Write from your actual process. Do not promise a next-day reply because it sounds reassuring if you cannot reliably deliver it. If you state a response window, confirm that it is current and explain relevant working-day limits. If you do not have a dependable window, describe the next action without inventing a deadline.

Keep essential information here rather than hiding it inside a long FAQ. The visitor should not have to guess whether sending the form reserves a date or commits them to paid work.

Replace “Tell us more” with a useful prompt

A wide-open message box can leave people wondering how much to write. A short prompt can give them a starting point.

Consider a fictional painting company that offers written quotes after discussing the job. Its message prompt could say: “Tell us which rooms or surfaces need painting and the town or city where the work is needed.” The business would need to confirm that this matches its own quoting process.

That prompt is more useful than “Describe your dream transformation.” It asks for information the person can supply without preparing a specification or knowing the final price.

Avoid turning the first message into a full project questionnaire. Review each requested detail: will the business use it to respond now, or could it wait for the conversation? A budget question may be relevant to some services; if it is optional or an estimate is acceptable, say so rather than leaving the visitor to guess.

Make labels and instructions work together

W3C’s form-label guidance explains that controls need labels that identify their purpose and are properly associated with them. A field that looks labelled is not enough to establish that the implementation is accessible; ask the site maintainer to check the association.

Use plain labels such as “Email address” rather than relying on text inside an empty field. W3C’s form-instruction guidance recommends explaining required and optional information and relevant formats. It also says placeholder text is not a substitute for a label.

Keep a helpful prompt visible beside the field. If your audience includes people in both Canada and the US, check that wording and format rules fit the locations you actually serve. Do not require a US ZIP code when a Canadian customer is expected to use the same form.

Ask only for information appropriate to this contact route. Do not invite passwords, payment-card details or private case information into an ordinary enquiry box. The wording should reflect how your business actually handles enquiries, rather than making broad privacy promises you have not checked.

Use specific facts instead of reassurance without substance

“You’re in good hands” does not explain what someone can expect. A useful contact section might instead identify the service area, explain whether the next step is a discussion or a quote, and point to the relevant scope.

For the fictional painting company, a clearer introduction could be: “Ask about a painting quote. Tell us where the job is and what you would like painted. We’ll discuss the work before any appointment is confirmed.” This is an illustrative rewrite, not a statement about a real provider.

Do not add an award, credential, testimonial or availability claim just to make the form feel more persuasive. Use facts you can substantiate. Google’s people-first content guidance encourages useful, reliable information and clear sourcing; it does not establish that a particular contact-page phrase will increase enquiries.

Read the confirmation as part of the same conversation

Review what visitors see after a successful submission. Does it acknowledge the action they took? A quote request should not produce a message saying an appointment is booked unless the system really completed that booking.

Also review error wording with the site maintainer. Can someone understand which detail needs attention and how to correct it? Rewriting the confirmation does not fix a delivery problem. If you test your own form, use an agreed test message and confirm receipt through your normal process. Avoid sending invented enquiries to other businesses.

Check the whole section on a phone

Read the heading, instructions, labels, button and confirmation on a narrow screen. Look for clipped text, instructions that disappear, and a button label that no longer explains the action. Use a keyboard on a computer to check that you can reach the fields and see where focus is.

These checks are a starting point, not a complete accessibility audit. Record a wording problem separately from a technical fault so each gets the right fix.

Finish with one small, testable change

Choose one mismatch: a misleading button, an unclear message prompt, or a next step nobody has explained. Record the old wording, the replacement, the reason and the date you checked the result.

The immediate finish line is a clearer, accurate enquiry route. If you later review enquiry numbers, consider other changes and normal variation before attributing a difference to the new wording. No enquiry volume, search ranking or AI inclusion is guaranteed.
