A Simple Internal Link Audit for a Small-Business Website

By Harmon Content · Published 21 October 2026

Someone finishes a useful article on your website. They understand the problem, but cannot find the service that would help. Before writing another post, check the route between the pages you already have. A small internal link audit can turn that dead end into a clearer next step.

Start with one important service page and a few related articles. Check what each reader needs next, whether the link describes that destination, and whether the destination delivers. Fix the weakest route first. You do not need to add a link to every paragraph.

This is a practical review for small service businesses in Canada and the US. The examples are fictional planning situations, not client results or a promise of better rankings.

Start with three pages and one decision

Choose a service you actually want enquiries about. Open its service page, one older article about a related customer question, and your contact or quote page.

Write down the decision at each stop: “Is this the help I need?”, “What does this business offer?” and “How do I ask about my situation?” If two pages have the same job, the issue may be their content rather than a missing link. Our guide to choosing between a service page and a blog post can help with that distinction.

Use this small sample to find a specific problem. You can review more pages once the first route makes sense.

Follow an older article as a new visitor

Start at the article itself, without going through your homepage. Read the opening, scan the headings and reach the final section. Can you find a sensible next step without opening the navigation menu?

Imagine a commercial cleaning company with an article about preparing an office for a first visit. A reader may next want to check the company’s office-cleaning scope. A contextual link can help them do that. Sending everyone straight to a quote form skips the information they may still need.

Another reader may only need the checklist. Let the article answer that question fully; do not withhold useful instructions to force a click. Add a service link where it helps, rather than turning every section into a sales pitch.

Make the label match the destination

Compare these fictional options:

  • Too vague: “Learn more.” The reader has to guess what comes next.
  • Misleading: “Book your cleaning” when the destination only accepts quote requests.
  • Specific: “See what our office-cleaning service includes” when the page explains that scope.

Google’s link best practices recommend concise, relevant anchor text with useful surrounding context. Avoid forcing a list of keywords or locations into the label.

Descriptive labels also support accessibility. W3C’s explanation of link purpose describes how people should be able to understand a link from its text or its associated context. Read your labels aloud without the rest of the page. Would you know which one to choose?

Test the destination, not just the click

Open the link. Check the page title and the first useful section, rather than stopping because something loaded.

  • Does the page describe the service the article mentioned?
  • Do its service area and practical limits match the reader’s situation?
  • Is the offer current, with a clear way to enquire?
  • Does the link land on the intended page rather than the homepage or an error page?

For a business serving only one Canadian province or US state, a relevant article does not make the service available everywhere. Keep the destination honest about where and how the business works.

Google generally extracts crawlable links from HTML anchor elements with an href attribute. If your editor creates a clickable card or button, ask whoever maintains the site to confirm it produces a proper link to the intended URL. A visual click test alone does not establish that.

Check the route into an important page

Now open the service page and work backward. Which existing page sends a reader here? Google advises linking to each important page from at least one other page on the site. There is no ideal link count to hit.

For this audit, distinguish “reachable somewhere in the menu” from “offered at the moment this reader needs it.” An article-specific link may provide a clearer route even when the page is already in the navigation. That is an editorial judgement, not a requirement to repeat the same link everywhere.

The reverse route can help too. If a service page briefly mentions preparation, link to the detailed checklist beside that explanation. Keep essential buying details on the service page; readers should not have to hunt through articles to understand the offer.

Keep a short change record

Use a note or a spreadsheet with these fields:

  1. Starting page: The article or page URL you reviewed.
  2. Reader’s next question: The reason another page would help.
  3. Destination and label: The actual URL and the visible link wording.
  4. Finding: Missing link, vague label, wrong destination or no change needed.
  5. Action and retest date: What changed and when you checked it again.

For the cleaning example, the next question might be “Does this company clean offices like ours?” The action could be adding one link to the relevant service scope. If the service page is unclear, record that separately. More links will not repair a confusing offer.

Keep customer names and private enquiry details out of this record. You need the recurring question, not the person’s identity.

Retest on a phone and with a keyboard

Follow the same route on a narrow screen. Check that the link remains readable in context, text does not require sideways scrolling, and nearby links are easy to tell apart. On a computer, use the Tab key to reach the link and Enter to open it. Check that you can see which link has focus.

Retest the destination after any change to its address. Do not submit a contact form just to prove the link works. These checks can catch obvious problems, but they are not a complete accessibility audit.

Set a useful finish line

For this small audit, success means you can follow the intended route, understand each link before opening it, and reach the right information. Keep a dated record of that result.

If you already collect relevant analytics, you can later review destination visits or enquiries alongside other changes. This guide provides qualitative planning advice, not measured keyword volumes or traffic forecasts. Do not treat a short-term movement as proof that a link edit caused it.

For Google’s AI features, this is still basic website work. Google’s AI-search guidance includes internal discoverability among its usual SEO practices; it does not require a special AI linking scheme. Clearer routes do not guarantee crawling, indexing, rankings or AI citations.
