What is GEO? A Practical Guide for Small Businesses

By Harmon Content · Updated 9 October 2026

GEO means generative engine optimization: work intended to make a business’s content easier to discover and use in AI-generated answers. For a small service business, the useful starting point is still a website that explains what you do and helps a customer take the next step.

The practical answer: explain your services clearly, answer real customer questions and make the information easy to find. Treat AI visibility as something to observe, rather than an outcome a content provider can promise.

Three terms, one customer trying to choose

SEO: search engine optimization
Improving a website so search engines can discover and understand its pages, and visitors can find useful information.
AEO: answer engine optimization
A term used for making information useful in answer-based search. A clear answer to a real question is a sensible writing goal.
GEO: generative engine optimization
A term used for visibility in answers generated by AI systems. Different platforms have different ways of finding, selecting and presenting sources.

The same person might search for a service, ask an AI assistant about their options, then visit a business’s website. The page still needs to answer their questions when they arrive.

What Google actually says about AI search

Google’s current guidance says its usual SEO practices remain relevant to generative search. There is no special AI file or markup requirement for Google visibility, and following the guidance does not guarantee indexing or inclusion. That guidance is about Google; it should not be treated as a specification for every other AI platform.

Start with the page closest to an enquiry

Imagine a homeowner looking for help with a garden. “Outdoor solutions for discerning clients” tells them very little. A page describing seasonal maintenance, the tasks included, the service area and how to request a quote gives them something they can use.

Before adding more articles, check whether your main service page answers these questions:

  • What work do you do, and who is it for?
  • Where do you provide it?
  • What is included, and what needs a separate quote?
  • What should a customer tell you when they enquire?
  • What happens after they contact you?

Specific details should come from the business. Don’t invent coverage areas, certifications, availability or customer results to make a page sound more impressive.

Add useful detail where it belongs

A short FAQ can clarify a booking process. A separate article can explain a decision that needs more space. Link that article back to the relevant service, so the reader can continue naturally.

For example, a garden-maintenance business could explain the difference between seasonal tidy-ups and recurring care. The service page can describe the options briefly and link to that explanation. There is no need to create several near-identical pages for every wording of the same question.

Measure business progress, not an acronym

Keep a record of the questions customers ask, the enquiries you receive and which pages they mention. Where you have Search Console access, review search activity alongside those enquiries. A spot check of an AI answer can be an observation, but it is not a reliable forecast of next month’s leads.

The useful question is whether your website gives a potential customer enough accurate information to decide to contact you.
